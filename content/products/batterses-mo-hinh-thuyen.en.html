The BATTERSEA model (listed on our site as BATTERSES) recreates a British coastal cargo ship with a very distinctive layout: the engine room, funnel and crew accommodation are all placed at the stern, leaving almost the whole length of the hull for long cargo holds. The name Battersea – a riverside district of London on the Thames – and this hull form recall the coal-carrying coasters that were a common sight around the British coast for much of the 20th century.

Background: colliers on the Thames

For decades, the power stations and gasworks along the Thames in London needed huge amounts of coal, most of it shipped by sea from the coalfields of north-east England. To meet that demand, electricity and gas companies ran their own fleets of coastal colliers. Some were specially designed with masts, funnels and wheelhouses that could be lowered so the ships could pass under the low bridges of the river – they were known as “flatirons” or “flatties”.

Battersea Power Station, famous for its four white chimneys, was one of the places that received coal this way. By the mid-1980s, coal no longer needed to be carried up the Thames, and these ships gradually disappeared. Maritime museums in Britain still keep many models of coastal colliers as part of London's industrial memory.
